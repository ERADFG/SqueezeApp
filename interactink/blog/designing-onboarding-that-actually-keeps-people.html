Designing Onboarding That Actually Keeps People

InteractInk Blog · 34 min read

Most communities lose the majority of the people who will ever leave them in the first ten minutes after joining, not the first ten weeks. This is an uncomfortable number for founders to sit with, because it means the moderation policy, the events calendar, the long-term culture work — all the things founders spend the most time thinking about — matter enormously for the members who stick around, but almost none of that ever gets the chance to matter for the much larger group who quietly bounce before any of it becomes relevant to them. If a community is losing people, the highest-leverage place to look first is almost never the deep culture. It's the first ten minutes, and specifically, what a brand-new member is asked to do, see, and decide during that window.

The default onboarding is usually just a wall

The most common onboarding sequence, especially in smaller or newer communities, is essentially nothing at all: a member joins, sees a welcome message that was written once and never updated, sees a rules channel, and is left to figure out on their own what to do next. This isn't a design choice so much as an absence of one — founders are usually so focused on the substance of what the community is about that the actual mechanics of what happens in someone's first ninety seconds inside it never get deliberate attention.

The cost of that absence is larger than it looks. A new member facing an unstructured space with no clear next action defaults, overwhelmingly, to doing nothing — not because they're uninterested, but because the first action in an unfamiliar space is always the highest-friction one, and without a specific, low-effort suggestion for what that first action should be, most people simply don't generate one on their own. They read a few messages, maybe scroll a channel or two, and close the tab, fully intending to come back later, at which point the app's normal gravity — notifications from other apps, competing priorities, simple forgetting — takes over, and later rarely comes.

What a first ten minutes needs to accomplish

Effective onboarding accomplishes three things in quick succession, ideally within the first couple of minutes a new member spends in the space: it gives them a specific, low-effort first action; it shows them evidence that other real people are actively present; and it gives them a way to be seen by at least one other person, even briefly, before they've done anything themselves. Miss any one of these three and retention drops meaningfully, even if the other two are handled well.

The specific first action matters because "explore the space and see what interests you" is not actually an instruction — it's an absence of one, dressed up as an invitation. A far more effective first action is something narrow and concrete: "introduce yourself in three sentences: what brought you here, what you're hoping to find, and one fact about yourself that has nothing to do with the topic." This is low-effort enough that almost anyone can complete it in under a minute, specific enough that it doesn't require exploring the whole space first to figure out what to say, and — critically — it's a type of post that reliably gets replies, which brings us to the second requirement.

Evidence of other real, active people matters because a new member's very first impression of a community is formed almost entirely from whatever is immediately visible when they arrive, not from anything they're told about the community's actual size or activity level. A community with two hundred members but a quiet-looking general channel reads, in that first impression, as smaller and less active than it actually is. A dedicated, consistently active introductions channel solves this efficiently, because it's specifically designed to always have recent activity — every new member's own introduction adds to it — which means it's one of the few parts of a community that reliably looks alive no matter how quiet other channels might be on a given day.

Being seen by at least one other person, even briefly, before doing anything themselves, is the third piece, and it's the one founders most often skip. A new member who posts an introduction and gets zero response within the first hour has, in a very real sense, had their fear about the space confirmed — that this is a place where speaking up doesn't get you anything back. A new member who gets even one warm, specific reply within a few minutes has had the opposite experience, and that single reply does an outsized amount of work in determining whether they come back a second time.

The welcome bot problem

A common attempt at solving the third requirement is an automated welcome message — a bot that posts a generic "welcome to the community!" the moment someone joins. This solves almost none of the actual problem, because a bot reply doesn't read as evidence that a real person is present; if anything, an obviously automated welcome can make a space feel less alive, not more, because it signals that the actual humans in the community aren't paying close enough attention to greet new arrivals themselves, and a bot was deployed to paper over that gap.

Automated welcomes aren't worthless — they're useful for surfacing practical information, like where to find the rules or how to introduce yourself — but they shouldn't be mistaken for the human warmth a new member is actually looking for evidence of. The far more effective version of this is a small, rotating group of existing members, ideally drawn from your founding cohort or early active members, who've explicitly committed to checking the introductions channel regularly and replying with something specific and genuine to every new post that shows up there. This is real, ongoing work, and it's exactly the kind of unglamorous labor that determines whether a community's retention numbers are good or mediocre, far more than any feature or bot ever will.

Reducing the number of decisions before the first action

Every additional decision a new member has to make before completing their first meaningful action is a place where some percentage of people drop off, and this compounds faster than founders tend to intuit. If a new member has to first read a lengthy rules page, then figure out which of fifteen channels is the right one to post in, then decide what tone is appropriate, then finally write something — that's four separate decision points, each with some dropout rate, and the combined dropout across all four is considerably higher than any single one would suggest on its own.

The fix is to collapse as many of those decisions as possible into a single, clearly signposted path. A pinned message at the very top of the first channel a new member sees, explicitly saying "start here: introduce yourself in [specific channel] using this format" removes the channel-selection decision, the format decision, and much of the tone-uncertainty in one move. This isn't about making the community feel rigid or over-produced — it's about recognizing that decision fatigue is real, that new members have the least context of anyone in the space to make these decisions confidently, and that removing unnecessary decision points at exactly the moment someone has the least confidence to make them is one of the highest-leverage, lowest-cost things a founder can do.

Timing the first substantive prompt

Beyond the introduction, new members benefit from a second, slightly more substantive prompt somewhere in their first day or two — not immediately, which can feel like pressure, but not so late that the initial momentum from joining has fully dissipated. This might be an invitation to a specific ongoing discussion that matches something they mentioned in their introduction, or a direct, personal message from a moderator or founder referencing something specific from their intro post. The specificity matters enormously here; a generic "hope you're settling in!" does very little, while "saw you mentioned you're into [specific thing they said] — there's actually a thread about that from last week if you want to check it out" gives a concrete next action tied to something the new member has already expressed genuine interest in.

This is, again, real ongoing labor, not a one-time setup task, and it's the kind of labor that scales poorly by hand once a community grows large enough that founders can't personally track every new member's introduction. At that point, the right move isn't to abandon the practice — it's to formalize it into a rotating responsibility among a small group of active members, each covering a portion of new arrivals during a given week, so that no single person burns out trying to personally welcome everyone, but the practice itself survives the community's growth rather than quietly disappearing the moment it becomes inconvenient for one person to keep doing alone.

What to measure instead of just member count

Total member count is a genuinely poor signal for whether onboarding is working, because it counts everyone who's ever joined, including the large share who joined once and never engaged again — exactly the group a good onboarding process is trying to convert into something else. A far more useful number to track, even informally, is the percentage of new joins who post at least once within their first 48 hours, and the percentage of those who post a second time within their first two weeks. These two numbers, tracked even loosely over time, tell you far more about whether your onboarding changes are working than watching your total member count climb, because member count climbs regardless of whether onboarding is any good — it only reflects acquisition, never retention, and acquisition without retention is exactly the trap that kills otherwise promising communities.

If the 48-hour first-post rate is low, that's a signal the first ten minutes aren't giving new members a clear, low-effort action or enough evidence of real presence to act on it. If the two-week second-post rate is low even when the first-post rate is healthy, that's a different signal — it usually means the introduction got a reply, but nothing afterward gave the new member a reason to come back a second time, which points toward the second prompt described above rather than anything about the introduction flow itself.

Onboarding is never finished

The instinct once an onboarding flow feels like it's working is to treat it as solved and move attention elsewhere. In practice, onboarding needs periodic revisiting as a community's composition changes, because what worked for onboarding your first fifty members, who mostly arrived through personal invitations from people who already understood the space, often works considerably worse for member three hundred, who arrived from a much colder, more anonymous channel and has far less context walking in. Revisiting your introduction prompt, your pinned start-here message, and your first-day follow-up practice every few months, checking whether they still make sense for however people are actually arriving now, is a small, recurring investment that keeps paying off for as long as the community keeps growing and changing who's walking through the door.
